This was our 2nd time climbing together and my 7th time climbing in the Valley. A perfect fall weekend in the perfect fall place, enjoying good rock, pizza and beer amidst changing colors and a full moon.

## The Plan

Juan and I met on mountain project in late August of this year. We climbed Mithril Dihedral on Mount Russell together, a trip which I still am hoping to write a retroactive post on this blog for. We moved incredibly efficiently together, completing the long day of hiking and climbing in a very reasonable time. Shortly after Juan reached out with a couple climbs he had on his list, and we made plans to climb together again soon.

The Northeast Buttress of Higher Cathedral Rock is a route up one of the taller formations in the Valley, directly across from and nearly level with El Cap. It's a historic route and known for great quality rock and jams, as well as a high concentration of burly and exhausting wide climbing through chimneys and full-body squeezes. The route had been suggested to me as a good stepping stone to the Steck-Salathe, another historic and brutally squeeze-y climb in the Valley. Juan mentioned that the route had been on his todo list for some time, and soon we were setting a weekend on the calendar.

## Day 1: Northeast Buttress Higher Cathedral Rock | 10/24

After driving in to Camp 4 in Yosemite the night before, I was up with the late fall sun around 7 am and feeling well rested. The online reservation system currently in use makes it fairly easy to secure a site, and is much less stressful than racing in the night before to camp by the kiosk in hopes of a site. 

Juan was driving in from his home in Bakersfield this morning, and had texted that he would be arriving around 7:30. I hopped into the truck and cruised down the meandering road through the valley floor, grateful for the short-lived oasis of warmth inside the car. I was headed towards El Cap Meadow, where I parked the truck and boiled water for some oatmeal, looking around for Juan's white sedan.

Soon Juan was parked next to me, and we quickly sorted gear while I slurped down my breakfast. Right around our planned start time of 8 am, we were crossing the road and beginning up the trail to our climb. Higher Cathedral Rock sits behind and above Middle Cathedral Rock, which is the large blank face directly opposite of El Cap, across the meadow. To reach the base of Higher Cathedral rock climbers must hike up the boulder field to the left of Middle Cathedral Rock, from the Valley floor at just over 4000 feet to the base of the route at just below 6000 feet.

Although the gap between Juan and my climbing skill levels is vast, him being a much better and more experienced climber, hiking is an area where we both set an equally decent pace and I'm able to keep up with him. We snaked our way through the boulder field, attempting to follow as much trail as we could and negotiating annoyingly slippery rocks in the morning dew. Before long the route came into full view, illuminated by the rays of sun that had not yet reached the formations below. We could make out a party already on the route, and as we approached closer we heard the sound of a large piece of rock crashing down to the base of the climb. Making a mental note to put my helmet on as soon as we reached the base, we continued on.

At the base we quickly racked up and sorted our follower pack. We heard another party behind us, and were beginning to get nervous about traffic on this route that I had been confident we would have to ourselves, given its burliness, length, and approach. But Juan reminded me the Valley is always busy, and there's always people on the classics this time of year. Luckily the pair behind us were hopping on Mary's Tears and The Crucifix, a link-up of two climbs, the latter climbing a proud direct crack through the vertical headwall to the right of the corner that makes up our route, and up through the massive roof.

I led a first short pitch to a slightly awkward stance below a slung tree. In hindsight I should have climbed up through the three and walked 15 feet further to a big pair of bolts on a giant ledge. Juan led the next long pitch, through the tree and past these bolts, and up through blocky and somewhat loose terrain, to another stance that marked the top of pitch 2. From here I took the lead again, and continued up what was undoubtedly the most fun pitch for me on the entire climb, mainly because my lack of wide technique made the upper pitches a real slog. This pitch was almost entirely perfect 5.8-5.9 hand cracks, up vertical terrain and through a couple distinct roofs, before about 15 feet of 5.7 liebacking that I had run out of gear to protect. I was slightly nervous as I smeared my feet on the granite and pulled my way up, but looking back at pictures the fall would have been clean and fun.

At the top of pitch 3 I was already with the party we had seen from the base, who had seemed to be already a good ways up the climb as we were approaching. Luckily as Juan followed so did the follower of the party in front of us, leaving the belay we shared. Juan soon met me here, took the lead, and continued upwards. Pitch 4 was a short continuation of the same blocky vertical crack system, with the angle easing slightly until ending along with other slabs and flakes at the base of the vertical headwall, mandating a fifth pitch of a short sideways traverse along slabs and thin edges towards the base of the large corner that contained the upper pitches of the climb. Juan managed to easily link these two pitches together, and was soon back sharing a belay with the party ahead of us. I followed the pitch, Juan snapping some pictures of me as I came across the exposed slabby terrain.

Now at the top of pitch 5, we were again on the tail of the party ahead of us as their follower left the belay. None of us had been on the route before so we couldn't have known entirely what to expect, but in hindsight I wish we had asked to pass this party before they had started climbing pitch 6, as the next 300 feet or so of climbing consisted of a body-width crack, narrower in some places and wider in others, with very few decent belay stances or places to pass. They had set up a belay at a small stance on a flake about 60 feet above us. I took the lead and climbed up the blocky flakes that marked the entrance to the wide corner system. I had seen the leader of the party in front of us take a fall here, but I moved with confidence that we were moving more efficiently. Then, at only about 15 to 20 feet from the belay, I found myself pulling on a very small edge and looking for something above to grab or slot my fingers into. I felt one foot pop, and slide an inch down the wall as I pulled into the small edge with all my might. Inevitably, the second foot popped and slid as well, and I came falling back below the piece previously at my knees.

I was bummed to have fallen before even getting to the meat of the climb, and not really sure what had happened. After following, Juan said that he felt this section was slippery, much like the dew-ridden boulders below and certain other parts of the route; hidden in the shade 24/7 this time of year. I'll eagerly agree to massage my ego, and add that after pulling back up the rope and paying more attention to my feet I was able to negotiate the move without too much difficulty.

From here I was quickly back behind the party ahead of us. The stance he had set up in was barely big enough for one, I had a lot of rope left, and I could see the tree and sloping ledge mentioned by Steph Abegg as a better belay option than this one. Currently the leader of the party ahead of us had just crested over the ledge, where I assumed she was going to set up a belay. I asked if I could climb through, past the belayer in front of me and to the ledge the leader now stood at. He radioed his partner and she nicely agreed, allowing us to continue climbing. I called down to Juan to confirm he had heard the plan and then started off again. 

Now the climbing was getting burlier and more blue-collar, demanding full body exertion against the opposing walls of rock, with the small respite of a good hand crack in the back of the flaring chimney. I grunted my way up towards the small tree and ledge, and eventually pulled over onto it with good jams. When I did I was able to see that the leader of the next party had continued further on up, through the narrowing 5.9 squeeze I had read about and seen pictures of. In desperation to pass this party and not build an anchor in the middle of their pitch after they had let me pass, I got (literal) tunnel vision looking up this constricting slot in the rock, and decided I *must* join the leader of the other party at the stance above it. This ended up being a very bad decision for a myriad of reasons. Not the least of which being I ended up stealing the crux from Juan, who had obviously had dibs, being the one who had sought out this route and the much better climber (Sorry Juan). Though the more pressing and immediate reason quickly became the immense rope drag and lack of gear as I wormed my way up through the chimney. My first few pieces, now far below me, were not properly extended for such a long pitch, and worked to create an incredible amount of friction as the rope ran through them and the angling chimneys and ledge above. For me on the end of the rope, it felt like I had a 50 pound pack hanging off my waist as I wedged my knees and back into the rock, using my palms and heels to push against the wall in back of me. Near the top of the chimney is where the crux "squeeze" is, where a climber must take off their helmet in order to wedge their head upwards into the tightening slot. I spent a good five minutes here, a few feet below the leader of the party ahead of me, who was offering encouraging words as I screamed and grunted and said out loud to her "I cannot fit up through there." Juan told me later that another party across the canyon, somewhere on the Cathedral Spires, was shouting back grunts and screams, mimicking my suffering. I was so deep in the throes of my ill-advised lead that I never heard it.

Eventually I noticed a decent foothold below me, on the outside of the chimney. I planted my foot on it and reached around, finding a smattering of good holds along an angling slab. The whole time the move had been to exit the chimney on the outside rather than to actually "squeeze" through the entirety of the slot that marked the "5.9 squeeze". I've found that sometimes reading too much into beta for the sake of being prepared can often blind me to otherwise obvious movement, attempting to purely off-width a section marked as off-width or purely squeeze a section marked as squeeze.

Either way, I was now at the belay with the other leader, which was possibly just as awkward and small as the one I had made an effort not to share below. I was completely out of rope and gear except for a #5 cam, which I plugged into the wide slot, below the other leader's anchor. I belayed Juan up off this, him constantly having to scream at me to pull up rope, me deadlifting each pull of the rope, having to pull up about 50 pounds of weight with one hand just to pull free hanging slack out of the rope. The follower of the other party was climbing ahead of Juan, and as he neared the top of the squeeze chimney his partner offered to toss him down some rope to haul his backpack up. She did this, and was going to dock the bag when I heard "oh shit!" I was below her and looking down towards Juan when I felt the pack slide past my shoulders. The other leader and I both screamed at the same time, me screaming down to Juan who was just out of view in the chimney below. "Juan watch out!" As if there was much he could do in the situation. Luckily the pack went off the face slightly sideways rather than into the chimney, tumbled off the slab and down the face below, slamming into the rock multiple times and casting gear out into space with each impact. I heard Juan yell out in disbelief "What the fuck!". The sound as it hit the base was incredible, a boom across the canyon that you would think came from a two hundred pound block of rock, not a 15 pound back pack. People from across the canyon yelled out, asking if we were okay, asking what happened, "I thought that was...", I didn't hear the rest, but the imagination can fill it in. After checking that Juan was indeed okay and had not been hit, I called back that everyone was okay. Surely people were thinking who is this idiot screaming and grunting his way up a 5.9+ and dropping packs on people.

Now the party ahead of us had to bail, and they figured out their options. Juan took the lead to get us out of this poor jumbled stance with the other party and allow them to gather their things without us in their way. Shortly above Juan found a sling with a rap ring, and let the other party know of this before belaying me up to it. Now out of the mess and tangle of the other party for a short moment, I continued belaying Juan as he cruised up the remainder of the crack system, following jams and full body squeezes until eventually topping out at a ledge system.The other party belayed off the rap ring below me, again cramping the tight space. We wished them the best of luck and set off, and as I jammed my hands into the crack above and kicked my foot out to place it on a scoop higher up, I accidentally nailed this poor man in the helmet with my heel. I apologized profusely and felt very bad, and he said it was fine, but he was definitely not having a good day. Juan also told me later that a cam of his had popped off my harness earlier when first passing the other climber, and hit his helmet on the way down (Also Sorry Juan)

At the top of the crack system, now we were finally at a good stance and free of the mess and jumble of the traffic jam that had existed down below. The meat of the climb, the last 300ish ft of wide chimney climbing, had turned into a chaotic and poorly broken up period of climbing, no thanks to me and my over-ambition. Juan offered to take over the climbing from here, as we traversed out through blocky broken up terrain for a few final pitches to the summit. Fairly exhausted by the multiple pitches of wide climbing and seeing the afternoon dwindling, I was happy to let Juan take the final leads.

Juan led out sideways onto a cool blocky traverse, and up into the broken and bushy terrain to the summit. From here the going can be route-findy, as there are many different ways to go at many difficulties. We were looking for another squeeze chimney mentioned by many others, but followed a yellow line on an image marked "standard/original route" which took us up through widening jams, but never a squeeze chimney. Near the top we encountered one final steep section of crack climbing in a large corner, which began as perfect hand jams and gradually widened over a vertical 25 feet, becoming much too large at the top to jam without real off-width techniques. Here a large flake offered a small undercling to move upwards with, towards the ledge at the top. A short scramble from there and I was at a tree with Juan, the sun setting just as we made it to the summit.

The walk off was easy and casual aside from the wet boulders, reacquiring their dew and lack of friction as the darkness fell. I watched beneath my feet and managed to avoid stepping on several millipedes and even a newt. We fetched our packs from the base, unfortunately no cam, and made our way back to the car. After draining the two cold Pacificos from the cooler in my car, we did about a 30 minute tour of every road in the Valley looking for a place to buy more beer. Despite nearly every store having posted hours until 10 pm online, we came to store after store with 8:00 PM in big letters on the door. Eventually we sat down in Curry Village for some pizza and canned beer, expensive but thankfully open until 10 pm. We drank our beer and waited for the pizza, talking about climbing plans and life. Finally eating some food at around 9:30, the hot pizza was a god send in the cold fall night, and I shivered over my beer in two jackets as tourists walked up to the deck wearing short shorts. Probably from Michigan or something.

## Day 2: Werner's Ant Trees, The Surprise | 10/25

After a good night's sleep we were up again with the sun the next day. We spent a short while packing up our camp site and then discussed the climbing plans for the day. We had ambitiously talked about doing Serenity/Sons, a long and classic crack climb in the Valley as our Sunday climb. As Sunday came around however, I was feeling incredibly worked from our climb the day before. Serenity/Sons has been a climb I have wanted to do for a good while, but I have been somewhat putting off until I feel ready for it. Waking up on this Sunday, sore and dejected from the previous day's 5.9+, I felt very unready for Serenity/Sons 5.10d.

We talked about options, and I suggested a nearby climb I had also wanted to do for some time, called the Surprise. Juan looked into it some more and found a neat variation, which apparently avoided some poor quality lower pitches in preference of a more difficult direct line to the upper pitches. We agreed it was a great option, grabbed our packs and headed over.

This climb was at the Five Open Books area, and I specifically suggested it since it was only a short walk from our campsite. From the tourist-dominated Falls trail we quickly detour to the side and up into the trees, where several switchbacks cut the way up to the base of the climb. Several other parties approached in the woods with us, Five Open Books being home to many 2-3 pitch climbs, perfect for a Sunday morning outing. Another party mentioned they were headed to The Surprise, but after discussing graciously offered to switch to Commitment first, as they planned to climb both anyways.

At the base Juan took the lead and cruised up the difficult looking terrain. He follow a small crack up to a bulging wall which he stepped almost effortlessly sideways across, plugging a small cam before pulling over the side of the bulge onto bigger crystal-y footholds. Following up these moves I was in disbelief, it was a sequence that seemed to involve pulling into a tiny side-pull crimp and reaching over the bulge to grab... something? There were crystals and knobs but I wasn't strong enough to pull up on them in the way I needed to to do the move. I ended up having to plug the tiny cams back in and pull past the move.

From the top of this pitch I took the lead and led a very short second pitch. It was fairly easy 5.8 crack climbing, on lots of cool locker finger jams with frequent pods that would open up in the crack to create a good foot hold and allow for the easier grade despite the smaller jams. Despite this I quickly used up most of my finger sized pieces, and got nervous about the terrain above. I put in a belay and brought Juan up. I should have just kept climbing, the terrain wasn't too difficult and I could have figured out the gear, but I've begun to realize sometimes I have just have days like this. Where I'm tired and worked, and just not wanting to take a fall. I'm working on it.

Either way, Juan took the next lead and fired up the remainder of easy crack climbing, where he continued past a pair of bolts that marked the intended belay for the next pitch. This was the money pitch, the namesake of the climb, a surprisingly splitter finger crack up through the rock slab. Juan cruised up through this, making it look easy. I followed and worked my fingers into the rock, finding good stances and feet in several places. I really liked the climbing, moving along a vertical finger crack with brief sections of pumpy movement. To exit the crack and come over the top of the slab involved a tricky sequence. The crack sealed shut, with only a poor finger jam and a small sidepull appearing as the last offerings of the crack. Up and to the left sat a large rail, just out of reach for me, pulled in as high as I could on the sidepull. I desperately tried to find higher feet on the slick wall below, and eventually popped off and swung sideways. Now under the piece above me, I was able to pull up on some large holds, but unable to get back into the crack and try the move I had blown. I'll have to come back another time and lead it.

Once at the top we sorted our gear and walked off. Meandering by and greeting other parties topping out other climbs in the area, we made our way down the slabs. We were back at the car shortly, walking past a few friendly deer also enjoying their Sunday. Juan made some coffee as we parted out our gear and made sure we both had everything. We said our goodbyes and wished each other a good drive, and were quickly on our way. Climbing with Juan is always a great time, and is the closest to a climbing-mentor partnership that I have come, with how experienced and skilled of a climber he is. Climbing is such a vast and amazing sport with so much to offer, and I find it cool that someone as experienced and seasoned as Juan can still find excitement in climbs that are realistic for someone like me, so early and inexperienced in my climbing career. I can only hope and pray that when I'm as experienced and seasoned I'll be similarly skilled, and I'll get to go back and cruise through the burly climbs I never ticked off my list, with some green kid who is equally as stoked.
