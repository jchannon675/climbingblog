It's only fitting that this adventure be the first post on this blog as I couldn't fit nearly enough pictures or words into an Instagram post or text message to describe it.

## The Plan

The Subliminal Verses is a climb that had been on my todo list for quite some time. Put up in 2015 by legendary climber and alpine route developer Vitaliy Musiyenko, the route has quickly gained notoriety despite its extreme remoteness. The rock is said to be some of the best in the Sierra, similar to and rivaling the coveted granite of Charlotte Dome. Shortly after beginning multipitch climbing I stumbled across this route on mountain project and added it to my todo list, where it sat idle for about a year. Several months ago I was again scrolling mountain project, looking through the partner forums and planning out weekends. Evan had posted looking for a partner for a weekend I wasn't available, but mentioned in his post that he was stoked on Subliminal Verses as well. I sent him a message right away and we planned for a weekend in late September that worked for the both of us, a little more than a month out.

We talked briefly over the phone about ourselves as climbers and our plans and expectations for the trip. We agreed that the 17 mile approach warranted at least one other climb in the area, as long as we weren't feeling completely fried after Subliminal. We talked about several options, and decided the nearby Cherubim Dome made most sense as the day 2 climb. We were also very excited about the possibility of waking up early and hiking 5 miles around to the backside of Cherubim Dome to climb the moderate and massive Saber Ridge. We sorted out who was bringing what and both got work off, Evan was kind enough to get us both permits for the High Sierra Trail. We had to be at Lodgepole visitor center at 7 am to pick them up, and we elected to drive in the morning of to do so.

## Getting There

With a few sweet final hours of sleep in a bed and a very early wake up, I was on the road by 1 am and headed to the Sierra. I had never been to Sequoia National Park or Kings Canyon, and I was excited to explore a new area. The first few hours of the drive flew by as my body woke up, and soon I was on the I-5 flying past a sea of semi-trucks. At 2 in the morning hardly a single car is on the entire interstate, instead back to back semis fill the countless lanes and the horizon, kindly leaving only the left lane open. I stopped at the north end of Bakersfield for an early Jack in the Box breakfast and was overcome by the blanket of cow dung stench that enveloped the area. The General's Highway seemed to go on forever, weaving and winding up into the forest. I wondered what sights were hiding in the darkness.

I parked at the Lodgepole visitor center at 6 am, eagerly arriving a little earlier than intended. I noticed the small line of backpackers outside the visitor center already waiting, and laid back to nap until Evan arrived. 

Evan pulled in shortly after 7 am and we promptly picked up our permits and were on our way. The Crescent Meadow trailhead to the High Sierra Trail is just a short 10 minute drive from the Lodgepole visitor center, so we got back in our cars and headed that way. The drive takes you by Moro Rock and Sequoia's tree tunnel, as well as many massive Sequoia trees. We parked at the Crescent Meadow parking area, tossed our trash and donned our packs. We were hiking on the trail by 8:30 am.

## Day 0: The Hike In

We set a good pace, averaging around 3 mph. Quickly my massive pack was feeling very heavy and Evan was gracious enough to offer to divide the weight. I had brought a lot of food, Evan ended up carrying nearly all the climbing gear and was still much lighter than me. The weather was perfect and the views were amazing. Evan had hiked this trail many years previous all the way through to Mount Whitney and the Eastern Sierra. He told me how this trail had been built a century ago by a small army of men and horses hired with the single goal to punch a hole through this part of the Sierra. He pointed out Castle Rocks across the vast canyon to the right of us, another collection of supposedly pristine rock spires and domes with rarely visited summits due to their burly approach. The trail kept us high up on our side of this massive valley, formed by the middle fork of the Kaweah river. Eventually we passed a small ranger cabin at a campground known as Bearpaw Meadow, along with the remnants of what looked to be the old ranger cabin. Evan said this new cabin was not here the last time he was, 6 years ago.

From this point we dropped slightly down and then up into the valley below Hamilton Dome, following the High Sierra trail to Hamilton Lake. We passed over creeks and waterfalls, and past the smaller and less camp-able lower Hamilton Lake. On the opposite side of this lake from the trail looms the massive rock formation known as Angel Wings, so sheer and tall its presence rivals that of El Cap. The routes on this behemoth are a serious undertaking; long and hard. Too much for either of us at the moment, but we'll be back.

Just a few last minutes of laden hiking and we arrived at Hamilton Lake, eager to drop our packs. The setting is serene, a beautiful and calm alpine lake surrounded by massive granite peaks on all sides. Here the High Sierra Trail wraps around the lake and up through the steep mountains, eventually through an impressive tunnel, Evan says. Cherubim Dome looms to the left of this lake, a commanding summit high above most nearby peaks, perched atop what looked to be an endless sea of granite slab. The shore was busy with backpackers, almost every site full with people beginning their multi-day trek across the Sierra. Evan and I managed to each find a nice spot for our sleeping setup and went to the lake's edge for a quick swim. After drying off in the sun we pumped some water, made some food, sorted our gear for the next day and talked about a wake up time. Setting an alarm for 6, I crawled into my tent to read my book and drift off to sleep.

## Day 1: The Subliminal Verses, Hamilton Dome

Getting up before the sun, we had slept well and were energized and ready to get moving in the brisk morning chill. We boiled some water and scarfed down what we could stomach, then once again donned our packs, now thankfully much lighter, and started hiking as light broke. In front of us we had a very demanding approach, or so we had read on mountain project. The 2-3 miles and 2 thousand-ish feet of gain don't sound terrible, except for the fact that hardly any trail exists for the entirety of the hike. It began with some scrambling up a gradually steepening boulder field, which ended in a small vertical band of cliff. We continued along to the right of the base of this cliff line, looking for a weakness to punch through. At the far right end we found what we were looking for; a loose and steep gully lined with just enough boulders and trees for us to scramble our way up it.

After about 100 feet the gully dumped us on the hillside, above the cliff band and the long steep slabs to our right. The angle eased only slightly, and we continued to pry our way up through rock and vegetation. The quality of rock on the boulders alone was getting me very excited for the climbing we were headed towards. Eventually we crested the ridgeline of this hill-ish dome and dropped onto the backside, where we again circumvented steep cliffs until finding a gradual enough slope to descend into the glacial bowl between us and Hamilton. This bowl is quite spectacular; a vast sloping plain of granite lined with small oases of wildflowers and vegetation. The greenery seems to cut a natural line to the notch we are aiming at, and as we follow it we think we might see some of the first small signs of an attempt at a trail, long ago, or possibly just the repeated foot traffic from the time of development. Either way, the going is fairly straightforward for this section and we saunter across the bowl as the sun finally crests over the peaks behind us.

The notch tunneled us through into what felt like entirely different terrain, the gravel and granite previously beneath our feet now replaced by loose dirt and mulch, the treeline and vegetation suddenly becoming much denser on this south-west side of the dome. We descended downwards, attempting to locate our route. Surrounded by so many granite walls so close together, it was difficult to tell which dome was actually ours. I was convinced that a smaller dome to our immediate right after the notch was our target, and that the larger monstrosity of a dome in the back surely couldn't be climbable in so few pitches. As we drew nearer and reviewed the few pictures and beta we had, it became clear that the larger dome really was our destination. After leaving one pack hanging from a tree with some water and jackets left in it, we started the last bit of descent down towards the base of our route. Here the real bush whacking began, navigating through boulder fields covered with thick and tall stalks of what I now know to be called Bush chinquapin, but at the time we just referred to as "spiky balls". 

As we drew nearer to the base and could make out some features of the route, I was beyond surprised to hear Evan shout "There's people on it!" Sure enough, there were two small dots way up on the face of the dome, connected by a very long length of rope. We were beyond shocked to see this; we hadn't seen any other climbers yet and this route gets less than 10 ascents a year. Luckily we didn't have to deal with any traffic, just as we found the base the pair of climbers crested over the steepest part of the dome and onto the lower angle last pitches to the top, and we didn't see them again for the rest of the day. 

The beginning of the route is marked by a near perfect crack splitting a low angle slab that leads up to the steeper part of the south face. We located this without too much difficulty and started racking up. I was leading this first easy pitch and Evan was taking the 5.10b crux of the route on pitch 2, a menacing looking roof which we could see from where we stood. At around 10 am we tied into our ends of the rope, checked our knots, Evan got me on belay, and we began our first pitch of climbing together.

The first pitch went by quickly, an easy grade and straight forward climbing. Already I could feel my feet sticking to this high quality unpolished granite with a tenacity I hadn't experienced before. I built my anchor and belayed Evan as he climbed up below me. I handed him the large amount of gear I still had left after such a cruiser pitch, we agreed on how amazing the rock was, and then he set off to see what this crux had in store for him. Evan climbed steadily upwards towards the roof, clipped a bolt on the lip of the roof, and pulled the move without any visible difficulty. We hooted, Evan clipped in to his bolted belay, and then it was my turn. With a backpack on containing a couple small jackets and liters of water, I was nervous about pumping out following this pitch. But the climbing was surprisingly cruiser, giant knobs and pockets creating holds everywhere and anywhere you could want them. The roof was intimidating to arrive to, but with a little composure and a high foot I was soon over it, grabbing for the knobs above it. It was just about 11:30 in the morning and we were 2 pitches up with supposedly 8 more to go, and through the most difficult section of the climb.

From here the climbing never ceased to be spectacular, climbing straight up a nearly vertical sea of granite via big juggy knobs and positive holds. The next couple of pitches followed a broken crack system up towards another prominent roof. Evan was gracious enough to let me lead them both. The fourth pitch being the only other 5.10 pitch on this climb, it was my chance to get a bit of a crux in. The third pitch was slightly lower angle than the fourth, and was full of face holds and cracks to jam. Protection was available everywhere, and the climbing was mostly easy. Soon I was at the end of my rope and built a belay to bring Evan up. Almost every pitch on this climb is a full 60 meters, and the way we climbed it we often found ourselves using our entire 70 meter rope. Extremely long pitches of fun, direct climbing on fantastic rock.

The 4th pitch was significantly trickier, with some finicky moves on small feet and finger jams, but still nothing particularly exhausting or challenging. I made my way up to the roof, unsure of what to expect. When I got there I planted my feet on the featureless slab below the roof and reached over it, only to find the most featured and juggy granite formation I have ever seen. Like a giant brown granite sponge, full of huecos and rounded ribs of rock. I plugged a #2 cam into one of the huecos and monkey-barred my way across the roof. After hanging from the other side for a picture, I realized I hadn't extended my piece enough and after traversing sideways around the roof the drag was noticeable and causing the cam to walk out of its placement. I once again monkeyed my way back across the roof, extended the cam with a sling, and then traversed out the roof for a final time. So nice I had to do it twice!

From here the angle eased dramatically and we cruised up towards the top of the dome. We had read differing reports of this climb consisting of anywhere from 7 to 10 pitches covering 1500 ft of climbing. We reached the top of the route around 2 pm after only 6 pitches with a small bit of simul-climbing on the last pitch. Nearly hitting the end of our 70 meter rope on every pitch, it was likely around 1300 ft of climbing. From the top of the route the views were panoramic and fantastic, though the summit still stood a few hundred feet above us to the east, a small scramble taking us to the peak. The top of this dome forms an incredibly sheer ridgeline, the west side of the dome drops off completely vertically for thousands of feet.

At the top we took in the views, got our pictures and looked around for a summit register. Unfortunately there was none to be found, it would be a very cool mission for someone to take one up there. We ate some food and drank some water, then started looking around for the rappels. After a short minute of searching we found 3 sets of tat cord wrapped through a hole in the rock with rap rings hanging off them. We backed it up and threaded the rope through, set up the rappel and weighted it, took the back up off and started on our way down. Three quick and easy rappels took us down past bolted anchors to the ground of another notch.

From the notch the rappels had dropped us into, we had to circumnavigate the other dome dividing us from the notch we originally hiked in from. Here the bush whacking and spiky balls both again increased in intensity for a short period as we made our way down a gully and back to the tree we had stashed my bag at. My preferred method was brute force, and I cut a straight line through the bushes by jumping into pits of branches and spiky balls and unknown depths. Shortly we were back at the bag, collected our things, and began our long hike back. Once through the notch the going was fairly easy and incredibly scenic, now having the wherewithal and leisure to really take in the sights.

As we came over the hill-ish dome dividing us from our camp at Hamilton Lake, I could once again make out the small dots of the other pair of climbers, far below us on the walk off and almost back to the lake. I arrived at camp less than 30 minutes after them, and they were still sorting their climbing gear from the day. They had been there for several days already, had climbed a route on Angel Wings previously, and had hiked out the day before to camp at the base of today's climb. Tomorrow they were planning to hike out. 

Evan got back shortly after me, and we solidified our new routine of swimming, pumping water, and making food. More backpackers trickled into camp to fill the sites vacated that morning, and soon the sun disappeared behind the dome that we had spent the day climbing. Full and tired, I crawled back into my tent and picked up my book. 

## Day 2: What Dreams May Come / Dark Angels Have More Fun, Cherubim Dome

Evan and I had been toying around with the idea of climbing Saber Ridge this day. A very long and sustained ridge climb, it goes at a very modest grade for over 2000 ft of climbing, and is another one which has been on my todo list since I first started searching through alpine multipitch climbs on mountain project. The only issue is that it is located behind Angel Wings and Cherubim Dome, a little over 5 miles and 2000 ft of elevation from our current campsite. Ambitiously, we hoped to have the energy to wake up early Sunday morning and move our camp to this new location, giving us still many hours of daylight to complete the climb. As I crawled into my tent that Saturday night the reality of this early wake up and hike fully dawned on me. I climbed back out of my tent in my boxers, walked over to Evan's outdoor bed, knelt down and discussed. We were both very agreeable to climbing the nearby and more technical Cherubim Dome. In hindsight I'm very glad we did this; I will be back to the Saber Ridge area to climb it and other climbs around it, I would want to come back to this area for Cherubim Dome if I had not ticked it off.

The calm, inviting weather that had been a welcome companion on our trip shifted its character that Saturday night, and my tent shook and flapped throughout. Cozy in my shelter with my earbuds, I definitely felt sympathy for Evan who was cowboy camping outside under a nearby tree. We rose around 7 am to a white capped lake and gusts that funneled through the valley, shaking everything in it that wasn't made of rock. We discussed the poorer weather, the cloud cover and possibility of light rain. My Garmin weather forecast said 0% chance of precipitation, and that was good enough for us. We were headed out by 8 am for the day's climb. 

We planned to climb a route called What Dreams May Come on Cherubim Dome. This route was the same grade of difficulty as the one we had climbed the day previous, put up in the same year by the same developers. We felt very confident after breezing through yesterday's climb.

From the lake we continued on the High Sierra Trail for a short ways, switchbacking a few times up the hill. Eventually we scrambled up and out of the trail, around a ridge and into another glacial bowl at the base of Cherubim. The granite seemed to go on forever, the bowl even more continuous and vast than the one a day before. We slowly made our way up the slabs to a tree denoting the base of the climb, our legs now feeling the full effects of 3 straight days in the alpine. After leaving a pack at the tree, we kept scrambling upwards. The angle gradually steepened and shrubbery and loose rock disappeared, until eventually the terrain became too technical for us to move without a rope. We threw our gear on and Evan got started leading the first pitch.

This climb was denoted in guidebooks as having an "R" rating, meaning that even with a rope and protection, there are spots on the climb where if you fall you could get seriously injured or die. This happens because there will be sections of the climb where it is not possible to place gear and there is no fixed protection, creating a mandatory run-out where the leader has to go a certain length with no protection, taking a long fall all the way back below his last piece if he blows it. 

The validity of this R rating was still up for debate to us. We had read a comment from one of the developers stating that bolts had been added to the first 4 pitches of this climb, and that those pitches were now "fairly well protected". The same comment stated that if we weren't feeling up for the final pitches, which are a "bit spicy", we could escape onto a nearby climb of slightly lesser difficulty and much less commitment. We had an array of options and felt comfortable heading up the route.

Almost immediately the level of commitment felt starkly different to that of yesterday's climb. Evan led the first pitch and placed no more than 5 pieces of gear in his nearly 70 meter pitch. Granted the climbing was very easy on very featured low-angle rock, but a fall still would have been very consequential at nearly any point on the pitch. I followed my way up it and got ready to lead the second pitch. On the guidebook topo we had for the route, this pitch was shown as having 4 bolts. I took the full rack and started up the pitch, not realizing the 4 bolts would be my only protection for the next 70 meters. The first bolt was probably about 30-40 feet from the belay, and I had to get there largely unprotected. The climbing was 5.8 slab, straight forward and with positive edges, but still tenuous feet that felt like they could slip at any moment, and fingers that would have no chance to catch you on the miniscule holds they rested on for balance. I wrapped a sling around a small chicken head and continued upwards. Another 20 feet up, still at least 10 feet below the bolt, I made a desperate attempt to plug a #2 cam into a hueco much too large to hold it. It immediately walked out after moving past it and fell down the rope. No option but to keep going, I kept my head cool and finished the last bit to the first bolt. Sigh of relief. 3 more bolts loomed above, all with sizable distances between them. I kept on moving and managed to cruise up to the anchors without too much difficulty. This was about a 60 meter or 200 foot pitch of sustained 5.8-5.9 climbing, and it felt safe to fall on about 40 feet of it, the 10 feet above each bolt.

Pitch 3 was the first 5.10 pitch and supposedly one of the cruxes of the route. The crux had the same grade as that of the second pitch that we breezed through the day before, and we felt confident. Evan took the lead and headed up towards the steep section of wall that stood between us and the upper portion of the dome. Just before he got to the steepest section, I heard him call out for the first time yet in our partnership to "Watch me here." I held onto the rope as I watched him try to figure out the slabby moves. After a while he had to hang on the bolt protecting the crux, and attempt to figure out the moves to get past this section. After a good bit of trying Evan decided he couldn't figure it out, and aided past using a sling off the bolt. Once above it he made easy work of the steepest section, the wall surprisingly featured with holds and pockets. 

Soon it was my turn to follow and I started up the rock. This pitch was notably better-protected than the previous two, featuring several bolts and chances to place gear. The crux was very difficult, especially when compared to the 10b crux from the day before. An almost featureless and vertical section of rock, dotted with only a couple dishes large enough for a finger pad and a few millimeters deep. It seemed to be some sort of horrendous mantle up onto the lower angle slab on top, but I couldn't figure out the moves either. I pulled past on the sling Evan had attached and fired up to the belay.

Once both at the top of this pitch we discussed the rest of the climb. Both of us felt very surprised by the difficulty of the crux and the level of commitment on the lower pitches, and neither of us felt particularly excited about the strictly unprotected 5.8 R traverse pitch that stood in front of us. Escaping to the slightly easier and less committing route called Dark Angels Have More Fun sounded like a fine idea to both of us. The line for this escape pitch seemed to be the most direct and natural line from our belay as well, taking us up a well-defined water gully. I took the lead and climbed another almost entirely unprotected 70 meter pitch of 5.6 - 5.8 climbing. Eventually the water gully changed into a crack system, which I built a belay in the bottom of and brought Evan up to. He took the next lead and fired up the splitter low angle crack, officially putting us on Dark Angels Have More Fun.

This put us right at the right-most base of the imposing vertical headwall, poised to take advantage of a corner that formed a small weakness in the castle wall. We climbed upwards through the crack systems and flakes that compose the upper portion of the dome. I led the first pitch into the headwall, climbing a full 70 meters up the slab and onto the vertical buttress. The climbing was trickier than it looked from below; gear placements were very finnicky and some movement was tricky, using large rounded slopers or liebacking with marginal feet. Soon I had a belay built and Evan was climbing up behind me. 

For Evan's last lead he was taking us straight up through the headwall, looking for a "5.9+ splitter crack" to the top. After a little bit of route finding, Evan punched through to the top without any difficulty. I followed him up the pitch through bushes and boulders, eventually plugging a couple fist jams in a dirty corner crack, anything but "splitter". A small knob on top provided a hold to pull over on, and with a little oomph and not so much grace I was shortly standing on the top with Evan.

This summit was incredible, towering over most nearby peaks, including the massive Angel Wings. Off the backside we could see Saber Ridge and the other formations in the area, and talked about how we'll be back next year to climb them. From the front side the exposure was incredible, standing on top of the headwall felt like standing on half dome. We spent several minutes on the top, again ate some food and drank some water, then began our descent back to camp.

We navigated our way down the ridge, moving over boulders that likely have never had anything but snow touch the top of them. Large refrigerator sized blocks moved around underneath our steps, I stepped onto the edge of a car size block and the entire microwave size piece I was standing on disconnected. We moved very carefully and were soon back down onto the more stable and continuous slab of the glacial bowl. I commented that we should have brought a skateboard as we flat-footed and butt-scooted our way down the slabs. 

Evan went back across the slab to fetch our bag from the tree it had been left on while I continued down towards camp. We wanted to make good use of the several hours of daylight we still had left by moving camp as far as we could up the trail, back towards our cars, shortening the next day's hike out. I got to camp a few minutes ahead of Evan and started packing away all our gear. A Sunday night, it was the first time all weekend we had seen the lake's edge entirely empty. One lone backpacker arrived as we were packing up to leave. We said our hellos to him, our goodbyes to the lake, and were expeditiously on our way.

A beautiful sunset accompanied us as we hiked out, the last bit of orange dwindling to darkness at the far mouth of the valley. A large waxing gibbous moon rose over Hamilton Dome and lit up the night for us as we walked. We were both exhausted and hungry, and the pack felt like it might meld with my back and never be able to come off. We ended up putting in a demanding 5 miles, getting us back to Bearpaw Meadow around 8 pm. We set up camp near another group of backpackers, quickly made some food and laid down, falling asleep the minute the head hit the pillow.

## Day 3: The Hike Out

I was up and moving before my 5:30 am alarm the next day, and we were on the trail before light broke. We set a good pace as we talked about life, feeling well rested from the windless night. We cruised through the 11 miles back to the parking lot, now that the terrain was mostly all flat and easy going. In just a few short hours pavement and other signs of civilization started to appear, and before we knew it the packs were off and we were sipping on sodas from Evan's car. We said our goodbyes, promised to reach out if we find ourselves in the other's territory, and started our 5 hour drives home.

The grandiosity of a place like this and the magic of experiencing it is hard for our limited brains to make sense of. Small, meaningless apes with their little metal tools, thousands of generations in the making. Swallowed up by this mythical land of the giants, sharing a snapshot in time with those who have been there long before them and will continue to be long after. Mating their current physical forms together; sinking a 28 year old hand into a crack many thousands of years old, allowing the summit to be perceived, and felt. Long ago they were both something different entirely, and eventually the bonds that hold each of their physical forms in place will begin to break, and they'll each be something different again. But if it was all ever meant to be something, to look a certain way, it sure seems like this is it.
