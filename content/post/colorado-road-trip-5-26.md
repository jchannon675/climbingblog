Earlier this year my good friend Mason and I went on a road trip that had been over a year in the making, a culmination of our climbing and exploration endeavors together over the year prior. We spent 8 nights in the roof tent on the top of my truck, driving through desert heat and alpine snowstorms. We climbed everything we could along the way, when our hands and fingers weren't feeling like they were ready to fall off. We spent time with friends and family in Colorado, before Mason flew back to start a new job. I drove back shortly after, getting in some mountain biking with my dad and good friend Weston on the way back. Certainly a road trip of a lifetime, I'm excited to write this post about it.

## The Plan

Mason and I first met in the beginning of 2025, on mountain project. Mason had posted looking for a partner to climb the South Face of Cathedral Peak in Santa Barbara, a sandy and vegetated climb on a sandstone dome in the Santa Barbara front country. I had recently gotten into climbing, was trying to break into trad and multipitch, and specifically had been working my way towards Cathedral Peak. Mason's posting was fate.

After one bailed attempt as a result of a hailstorm, we came back a second time and climbed the South Face in March of 2025, the first real trad multipitch route for the both of us. Over the next year Mason and I were a climbing duo, breaking in to the world of trad multipitch on world class routes in new places like Yosemite Valley, Tuolumne, Eastern Sierra, Red Rocks, Joshua Tree, and Tahquitz. 

My dad and step-mom moved to Fort Collins, Colorado in late 2022. My brother and grandmother moved there soon after. I started visiting often, excited to be spending time between the two states and exploring Colorado. I had been excited for a while about the idea of driving from California to see and stay with my family in Colorado, and as I discovered the world of rock climbing that excitement only grew. Mason and I talked about the possibility of doing the road trip together and climbing along the way, and soon that possibility became a certainty that we were both taking off work for. For Mason it worked out perfectly; he found an exciting new job to start when we got back and ended his old job right before we left. We planned out every day and night, packing in as much climbing and as many amazing campsites as possible. I had bought a new truck in 2025, and a used roof tent that I spent a few weekends fixing up. It was big enough for the both of us, and Mason had no objections. 

## Day 1: Triassic Sands | 4/28

At this point Mason was living out of the back of his Toyota Tacoma with a camper shell. This made it easy for him to park his home outside my apartment the night before, and around 3 am I knocked on his rear window to wake him up. Soon we were on the road and headed east, the miles flying by early in the morning. We were headed to Red Rock Canyon outside of Vegas to climb a route called Triassic Sands. 

By 9 am we were saying goodbye to California and hello to Vegas morning traffic. A short drive out of town takes you to the long, rocky, meandering road to Black Velvet Canyon. Eventually the road shrinks to a trail, where a severely sun-stained sign warns climbers of the massive sandstone canyon walls in the background. This was my sixth time climbing in Red Rocks and my third time in Black Velvet Canyon. Previously I had climbed the routes Epinephrine and Dream of Wild Turkeys, both classic outstanding routes.

Triassic was supposedly no different in quality, only slightly shorter than those other routes. Perfect for a pit stop on our road trip. Supposed to be full of continuous cracks uncharacteristic of most routes in the area, and with a pitch rumored to be similar to the climbing in our future destination of Indian Creek, Utah, we felt this was a perfect start to our desert journey.

In late April the season for this climbing area was coming to a close, and the beating sun reminded us of that fact as we walked into the canyon. Fortunately this canyon is known for having the most comfortable temps this late in the season, with the high walls blocking out sun and trapping in the cold. This along with some lucky cloud cover allowed us to be nice and cool for our day of climbing, even wearing a thin jacket for much of the climb.

Even on a Tuesday there were a fair number of climbers in the canyon taking advantage of the cooler temps. I pointed out climbers on the routes I had climbed, and noticed some on a hard route next to the one we were headed to. Luckily enough there was no one on Triassic, though another party arrived at the base just as we began climbing.

Mason took the first pitch, a short low angle crack that got us up to a large ledge. From here I took the lead and stepped sideways off the ledge, straight into the crux of the climb. Reaching up into a perfect sandstone crack, a combination of finger jams and liebacking gets you through the steep exit off the ledge, and soon the crack expands to perfect hand jams, and the featureless wave-like face transforms to a featured ladder of footholds, letting you know you're through the crux. The perfect hand jams continue on and upward, the crack splitting the sandstone wall for what looks like forever. Although the climbing is straight forward, I was feeling the pump after the crux and had to take advantage of what rests I could find. Eventually two bolts appeared to the right of the crack, and my pitch was over. Hoots were exchanged and Mason followed his way up.

From here Mason led the next pitch, continuing up the awesome crack. I followed him up, and soon we were sitting at the base of the fourth pitch. This is the pitch that we had read is similar to Indian Creek, and the rock had changed dramatically. Now we were at the base of a massive corner, with wavelike walls of featureless sandstone, a stark contrast to the abundance of footholds below. A massive flake of sandstone jutted out of the corner above us, marked with a tic tac toe game of chalk. Climbers typically mark "death blocks", or blocks that you shouldn't pull on because they could dislodge and cause a death, with an x in white chalk. A series of climbers had obviously had their fun with this block.

The climbing was stellar and also starkly different than the rest of the climb, with the featureless slabby sandstone mandating slightly more genuine crack climbing. The pitch was sustained and at times strenuous, with perfect jams up the steep corner. Eventually the features reappeared on the rock as I climbed out of the top of the corner, and built a belay to bring Mason up behind me.

From here Mason led us on an easy jaunt up featured rock to the top of the route. We unroped and quickly scrambled over to the nearby Whiskey Peak. We checked out the summit register and left our names, ate some food, and then were on our way. We were making great time and had a straight forward direct descent back down to the car. After some Inn n Out we were driving out of the other side of Vegas, into entirely new territory for the both of us. Tomorrow's goal was Zion, and we were eager to make as much of a dent in the drive as we could. We were headed to an area called Logandale Trails, a small ATV and dispersed camping area I had found by chance when using Google Earth to look for a spot for us to camp in between Vegas and Zion. In satellite images I could make out the jagged rocks casting shadows on the swaths of sand, looking much more interesting than the dirt highway pull offs that the other options consisted of. 

We pulled into the area in darkness, although a rising full moon lit the surroundings so well that we could see all the formations around us. We were in a tiny canyon of sand surrounded by these jagged rocks, completely alone on this still and quiet Tuesday night. We set up a small fire in the sand and popped up the roof tent, and one by one climbed up and went to sleep, tired from a very full and successful first day of our trip.

## Day 2: Zion Cragging | 4/29

The next day we were up with the sun, nice and early at 5:30 am at this time of year in this part of Nevada. We would lose an hour of time on our drive in today, Utah being an hour forward from California and Nevada time. We drove through the northwest corner of Arizona, and into Utah, through cities and towns dwindling in size from St. George to Hurricane to Springdale. The massive colored walls of Zion started to appear around us as we drove into town. We had e-bike rentals to pick up at 8 am, and we timed our arrival perfectly.

In my obsessive research I had learned that the e-bikes were the preferred way to get around Zion by most, avoiding the hassle of park buses and having the scenic drive to yourself for a slightly steep $100 a person. Mason and I splurged for the luxury and we were glad we did, it was an amazing way to experience the park. It feels wrong to have a bus roof blocking the panoramic views on that drive.

Without any difficulty we were on our rentals with our climbing gear stuffed into the crate storage on the back, cruising down the road towards the entrance of the park. The views were only increasing in magnificence and grandeur as we motored down the road and into the scenic drive. Here is where traffic is limited to only the park buses, and the road is dominated by rental e-bikes, much of Springdale's local economy on display. 

The road follows the snakelike canyon, surrounded by massive walls on all sides. Massive swaths of green exist in this oasis trapped inside the canyon, some extending far out through forks in the canyon to other walls and peaks. We were headed all the way to the end of this road, where the canyon narrows so dramatically it becomes a foot-hike through a river, known as "The Narrows". 

The area at the end of the road is known as the "Temple of Sinawava", and is home to several standout climbs in the Zion area. Our destination was a climb fittingly named "Tourist Crack", just a short jaunt from the parking lot. After locking up our e-bikes we found a covert area to hop a fence and trudge our way up to the cliff.

At the base we took our time roping up, watching a pair of lizards go at it in a way I have never seen before. I think we were watching a disturbingly persistent male lizard not taking no from a very uninterested female. Maybe she had young nearby because despite the constant attacks she wouldn't just run off? It was encapsulating.

Mason was first, and took the long lead with only a couple hangs. Intimidated, I started up the climb, a long pitch of mostly steep, perfect hand jams. Towards the top the crack narrows to a sustained stretch of thin hands with marginal feet, before widening dramatically. I had read in comments that there was off-width climbing at the top of this climb, an exhausting form of climbing involving figuring out how to jam which part of your body into a wide crack and then somehow move upwards on it. This widening of the crack didn't seem to be exactly that, more of a large flare with good fist jams in the back. After this section the flare closed black into the perfectly cut crack, now significantly wider than any section below. I plugged a large cam in, and, thinking this must be the off-width section, stacked my hands on top of each other, back to back, and shoved them into the crack. I pulled my feet out of flare below and desperately looked for somewhere to put them. Sadly, I quickly pumped out and hung on the cam I had placed. After hanging and looking at the move, it looked to be an obvious lieback. I grabbed the edge of the wide crack and planted my feet against the slightly lower angle wall opposite. Pulling and pushing just enough to keep myself on the wall, I took a few steps upwards, then grabbed a big rail of rock and pulled myself onto a ledge. There were the big metal chains. Hoots exchanged.

From here we walked to just the other side of the parking lot and crossed the river flowing out of The Narrows. We plodded our way up the river bank to the formation known as the Pulpit. 

This formation towers next to the Temple of Sinawava, a standalone tower and smaller version of the massive buttresses lining the valley. We were headed to a two pitch route to the top of the formation that supposedly had been put up in 1967, some of the earliest development in Zion. The first pitch consisted of a slightly overhanging bolt ladder, and Mason and I were eager to try out some new aid climbing gear we had bought the day before at a climbing store in Vegas.

I took the lead and sauntered my way up the bolts with my colorful new ladders. Mason had a new ascending setup and was excited to learn to use it, and quickly figured out the basics coming up behind me. The second pitch consisted of a wide and jagged crack splitting its way up the vertical pillar. This pitch required some thought and grit, pulling on loose fist jams, large slopers, and small edges. Without too much grunting I was soon at the top, and fixed the line for Mason to again practice his jugging.

The summit was incredible, the panoramic canyon surrounding you on your platform in space. All day we had watched the influx of tourists from buses to The Narrows, noticing a few pointing up to us with their walking sticks. But now at the top of this pitch, I could see they were all watching us, a mob of tourists forming in the parking lot and now watching Mason jug his way up the pillar.

I crawled over a couple more blocks to the true top, and found a small summit register. I opened it up and left our names, then shoved it back into its place in the rocks, hidden deep from the effects of any rain or snow. Mason reached the top and disconnected himself from his setup. He took out his phone to get a picture of me on the summit, and as I threw up my arms for a victory pose, the crowd of tourists below exploded with a cheer. Smiling, we gave them a wave, and then it was Mason's turn to soak in the fans' adoration. He got his summit pics and we rappelled to the ground, another cheer erupting as we touched down to the ground.

As the sun rolled into afternoon it cast shadows over most of the canyon, cooling things down drastically. Mason made the shocking (to me) decision to walk back across the creek in his pants, giving himself a nice wet and cold pair of pants for the bike ride back. I have to commend him, I didn't hear him complain or even see him shiver once. I was certainly cold just in my shorts in the wind and had nothing but sympathy and respect for Mason as we zipped out of the canyon on our e-bikes. 

After dropping off the e-bikes and getting some food and disappointing Utah beer across the street, we were on our way to our campsite. We headed to a small spot up the road, another site I had found searching on Google. Entering a small network of dirt trails just off the main road, we crossed over a tiny creek and set up camp alongside it. Plenty of other campers lined the creek, in much more long term setups.

We made a fire in a small ring we found, and relaxed to the sound of the creek. As the sun went down the sound of the frogs erupted from the creek, and I realized I was going to be glad I brought my earplugs. Later at night, as I was sitting by the fire, I had to grab one of these frogs to stop him from jumping directly into the fire. This happened several more times.

## Day 3: Pine Creek Canyon Canyoneering | 4/30

The next day we woke with leisure, taking our time to let the sun warm up the valley. We were going to be in cold water all day and letting the sun come out was in our best interests.

We were headed to Pine Creek Canyon, one of the premier canyoneering adventures here in Zion, a place known to many as the mecca of canyoneering. Neither Mason nor I had ever done any canyoneering before, but we figured we could manage rappelling through some cold water with our wetsuits on. I had booked the permits months in advance, and we were excited for the day finally being here. We drove into the park, passing the scenic drive from yesterday and continuing up the switchbacking road into the cliffs. At one point the road plunges directly into the cliff wall, boring through the wall that lines the canyon in a historic and scenic tunnel. Large windows punched from the inside of the tunnel through the wall expose a moment of scenic views as you drive by.

Eventually the tunnel ends and you immediately pass over a small bridge. After this bridge we parked the truck, amid a flurry of tourists attempting to do the same thing. There apparently is a scenic and easy hike nearby, the foot traffic clearly indicates. We put our wetsuits on, surely looking odd to national park tourists who might have never heard of rappelling down canyons.

After being a bit of tourists ourselves and asking a nearby park worker for the correct way into the creek, we were walking off the end of the parking lot and down into the slot creek that passed underneath the small bridge. We hiked along the dry rocky riverbed, passing constrictions of the walls on either side and small sandy beds beneath us. Eventually we came to the first obstacle, a series of stemming and hopping around a small pool to the first rappel station.

From here the canyon constricts more and more, the walls on the either side grow taller, and the pools at the end of the rappels grow wider and deeper. We heard a party coming up behind us, echoing in the tight corridors of the canyon. We were using a spare climbing rope as our canyoneering rope, a thick 9.8 mm thick 70 meter long dynamic non-dry rope. This is all to say it might have been the worst rope possible for this application; the rope was heavy and long, soaked up water and became fatter and heavier, and stretched and bounced while rappelling. This slowed us down somewhat, and made for several grueling doggy paddles through cold pools while trying to pull and untangle a massive nest of rope. 

Either way we were figuring it out and moving along, and found ourselves in massive chambers with some of the coolest rock formations I've seen. The walls tower and close together above, sealing Mason and I in this other world of pools and beaches, with only a small sliver of light peaking through above. Eventually, through a series of rappels, these massive rooms and deep pools funnel down into a human sized corridor, snaking its way through the rock with the walls beside still extending far into the sky above.

The walls still seal out almost all light from above, and we make our way through what feels like a massive cave system. After many more rappels the walls above us started to peel back as the riverbed we walked through widened. Soon we were boulder hopping our way down through a long creek, jumping in every pool we could find. Frogs lined the rocks along the water and leapt in as we came around the corners, splish-splashing in the pools like pebbles.

At the end of our creek scramble, one of the biggest warmest pools so far greeted us. From there a short 30 foot trail took us back up to the switchbacking road we had driven in on that morning. Now full of tourists taking photos, we popped out of the bushes in our wetsuits again looking very odd. I had read about hitch-hiking back up the switchbacks and through the tunnel back to the car, but I thought with our wet clothes we had a better chance of just hiking directly up the switchbacks, cutting our way through to the tunnel. I hadn't paid enough attention that morning to know if pedestrians were allowed in the tunnel, and I had doubts, but I hoped. 

Almost immediately a ranger pulled up in his suburban and put his lights on as I scrambled up the hill. "Looking for a climbing route?" He asked. I explained to him our situation and he explained to me that unfortunately, pedestrian traffic was not allowed through the tunnel. We had to be picked up by a good samaritan in order to get back to our car. I assume if we had been waiting for long at the top the ranger might have offered a ride, but the good grace of outdoorsy people is not to be underestimated, and we were very quickly picked up by a nice young couple on a Utah road trip. 

They dropped us back off at our truck on the other side of the tunnel, where both the tourists and sun were now out in full force. We peeled off our suits and hung the sopping rope to dry, and cracked a couple more sad Utah beers. After enjoying the Zion sun for good few minutes, we packed up and were on our way back to our same campsite from the night before. Once back, I took my mountain bike off the back of my truck, excited to have some free time and energy to make use of it. Just across the road from our campsite was one of the original sites of the Redbull Rampage competition, and I rode my way over to check it out. A massive dirt buttress lined with ridges that drop down at varying angles, anyone who has watched the competition knows the iconic landscape. I meandered my way through the few sections of trail I felt comfortable on, and stared in awe at the massive ramps and berms.

The next morning we again rose leisurely with the sun and the sound of the creek babbling by. Today we were headed out of Zion towards Moab via the Scenic Byway, a meandering road through some of the most remote and beautiful parts of Utah. This would take us up the same switchbacks as yesterday, to the tunnel where we still had one very large goal to accomplish in Zion.

The Headache is a 3 pitch 5.10 climb on the outside wall of the Mount Carmel tunnel. Known for splitter continuous hand cracks again more akin to Indian Creek than anything else in Zion, we were excited to get on this classic and see where our crack climbing skills lie. Parking our car just a short distance before the entrance to the tunnel, we donned our gear and started walking up the road. We walked past the line of cars waiting to be allowed through the tunnel, it being too narrow for two-way traffic. The park staff working the line of cars inquired about where we were headed. We were headed to a very unique climbing route by the name of "Otto's Route", named after the first ascensionist, John Otto, who climbed to the summit in 1911. This is incredibly early for rock climbing, and his technqiues at the time were quite different from what is acceptable today, or even 50 years later in the 60s. Otto drilled and carved his way to the summit, placing metal rods and carving scoops for footholds to climb a man-made ladder to the top of the route. The metal rods have long since been removed, leaving deep pockets large enough to fit 2 or 3 fingers into, and the large scooped footholds. This creates a shockingly easy way up the striking formation, pulling on pocket after pocket.

After leading the entirety of Castleton it was Mason's turn to get on the sharp end, and he was stoked as he took off up the first easy pitch. I followed my way up to him, and we enjoyed the panoramic views on the summit of the surrounding canyon. We reflected on our second tower of the day, proud to have made it to the top of two striking formations in the same day. On Sunday we drove into Denver and visited with some of my good friends McKenna and Logan, who have an apartment there in the city. Another end to another large portion of the trip, I was sad to see Mason go. But I was excited for his new job waiting for him, and to tell him of the rest of my adventures I still had yet to have. ## Day 23: Head to Moab with Dad | 5/20

It felt good to be back on the road again, I was certainly a little homesick and was glad to be making headway back towards my home. I was also excited for the mountain biking adventures that lay ahead, and to spend some good time with my Dad. We drove up the I-70, back into the mountains I had emerged from just a couple weeks ago. Now not a speck of white remained, and rather the sun beat down as we cruised up the long highway into the mountains. Somewhere near the high elevation stretches of the road, I was lucky enough to see a mountain goat, large white and regal, standing not a few feet from the higway barrier and munching on vegetation. The hours flew by for me, now well-conditioned to long monotonous stretches of driving. Soon we were making the turn into Moab and heading down into the familiar canyon walls. 

We stopped at a small trail system known as Moab Brand Trails, a series of loops and single track through smalls hills and boulder fields outside of Moab. It looked to be a minimum-effort warm up day for us in Moab, and we were eager to pull the bikes off our racks and get to riding. We cruised through the single track, getting accustomed to the magnetic grip of the Moab sandstone. We cut back and forth across the hillside, looking for more and more difficulty in the trails. Ultimately we rode about 15 miles and 1600 feet of gain, working a little harder than we intended on our warm up day. We rode back to the cars along an asphalt bike path as the afternoon petered away. We drove back down the meandering Colorado River road I had camped on with Mason weeks before, this time being lucky enough to find an actual campsite unonccupied. We filled out the self-registration slip and posted it on our site, and retired into our respective vehicles for the night's sleep. My Dad had a new air mattress for his Subaru he was excited to use, and I found myself happier to be back in the tent than I had expected.

## Day 24: Moab Mountain Biking with Dad | 5/21

We had debated what we were going to ride for our one big day in Moab. There were tons of good options, and we were going to have stuff to come back for no matter what. I ended up convincing my Dad to join me on a long link-up of several "magnificent" trails, called the Magnificent 7, or Mag-7. This was rated double-black, with a supposed fear-factor section that involves walking your bike along a cliff's edge, past signs that warn of other bikers' demise. It seemed like a great fit for a father-son ride for us.

We were at the trailhead at 8:30 am, driving a ways up from our campsite along the river, to the plateaus on the cliffs above. We filled our pack bladder and donned our helmets and shoes, eager to hit the trail. We cruised along the first few sections of trail; fun flowy single track over sandstone and through small desert shrubs. On sandstone the trails would follow painted white lines, sometimes only observable at the last second, prompting quick changes of direction. We soon met another rider who accompanied us for a while and offered to take some pictures of my Dad and I. He was doing a link-up called Mag-5, which avoided the harrowing bit of exposure at the end. It also avoided miles of painstaking slogging, which we had yet to find out.

Getting passed by a group of e-bikers, we slowly pushed our way up the hillside, coming to many boulders and ledges that took a massive amount of effort to overcome, while already expended from the climb below and above. Eventually we popped out on the top of the canyon wall, overlooking the road we had driven in to Moab on. Here we merged through a pack of jeeps posted on the canyon rim, having followed one of the many offroading routes to the same location. We marveled at the views and snapped some pictures, my Dad telling me to get back from the edge.

I was getting fast and loose, coming back up on the pack of e-bikers, when I launched off a large rock, landed directly on the edge of another, and heard the pop and hiss of a burst tire. 

I had just replaced this tire after popping it while mountain biking with Conor a few days ago, and specifically paid more for the tire that was supposed to be more durable. Granted, I did slam it into a massive rock. Either way, I was pissed as I walked my bike down the last little bit of fun downhill. At the time I took little solace in the fact that it had happened so nearby the road, rather than miles into the desert plateau as we had just been. I walked down the hot asphalt road to our campsite, about a mile down, cursing the tire. Before long the van of the ebikers pulled up alongside me, one of them inside and graciously offering me a ride. I was very grateful, the stagnant sun more than I had expected. He dropped me at my site and told me of alternative methods to keep the tire from popping, and wished me luck. I hopped back into my truck and cruised down the road, looking for my Dad.

## Day 25: Arches National Park with Dad, Head to Zion | 5/22

The next day my Dad and I were properly exhausted from the day before. We opted for an easy morning exploring Arches National Park together, a nice change in pace from the demanding mountain biking we had been doing together. We drove in past the toll booths, where they made me renew my America the Beautiful Pass, my expired pass from April of last year almost having gotten us through the entire trip. We drove up the winding road, past various towers and buttresses, and of course, arches. We pulled over a few times to snap some quick pictures of the horizon, and I pointed out landmarks from the map as my Dad drove. We were headed towards Delicate Arch, one of the largest attractions in Arches National Park.

I had watched The Dark Wizard, about Dean Potter, during my stay in Colorado and had been particularly moved by it. I was excited to see this area that had been such a defining moment in Dean's life, for better or worse. I droned on about Dean and the documentary to my Dad as we parked in the filling lot and made our way up the trail. It was only a few miles to the arch, but up a steep sandstone slab covered with tourists. We made our way up, and followed the stains of foot traffic behind some large domes. Eventually the trail narrowed and steepened into a perfectly flat and narrow sidewalk-like ledge that wrapped around the backside of a sandstone dome. We awkwardly made our way through the trove of tourists, some scrambling off-trail up exposed slabs in sandals, others gripping for dear life to the wall three feet from the edge. As we rounded the dome the arch came into full view, as did the line of people waiting to get a picture in front of it. We sat and admired the arch for a while, and I snuck up to examine the line that Dean had climbed. I thought I could make out the faint scars of some very controversial rope marks.

## Day 26: Zion Mountain Biking with Weston | 5/23

Weston and I have known each other since high school, but only became good friends several years ago in our mid-20s. We both loved to adventure in the Santa Barbara country, and we did many trail runs and mountain bike rides together. Weston had known about my travel plans and managed to plan a work trip to Arizona around the same time, intersecting me in Moab for a day of mountain biking. We had caught up around the campsite the previous night, and shared some laughs before getting a good nights rest, and now we were eager to start the day. For me, it was the last day of activity on my trip, and I was ready to give it everything I had left in the tank.

We started with a nearby and popular loop called Whole Guacamole. When I had picked my bike up from the shop in Moab, the clerk had told me "your shift pod knob bolt is stripped." I'm not sure how it would be or why you would have to access the shift pod bolt to change the rear tire, but he assured me it would last for another day of riding, as I told him this bike just had to last me one more day in Zion.

Sure enough, maybe a few hundred yards into the trail, my shift pod fell into my wheel and started banging around in my spokes. Screw Poison Spider Bicycles. After going back to Westons truck and afixing the shifter with a large amount of tape, we were ready to roll. The tape job ended up staying for several weeks and more rides later.

This trail was very fun, and reminiscent of the ones I had ridden in Moab, as it snaked along sandstone slabs following painted lines, and climbed over improbably steep boulders that stuck like glue to the rubber tires. Soon enough we had completed the 11 mile loop, still before noon. We put our bikes back on the car and looked for our next option, debating what to ride.

We ended on a large loop called Hurricane Rim Loop, a ride with prestigious "International Mountain Bike Assocation EPIC" status. The large loop is slightly west of Zion park, east of the town of Hurricane. We parked on the large empty plains outside and above the town, the wind swept horizon dotted with only a few quiet trailers. We started riding into the distance, following the edge of a large canyon as it cut into the plateau. Eventually we forked back into the plains, riding up flowy single track for a few miles. The single track seemed like it would be exceptional going the other way, and it wasn't long before we realized we had taken a wrong turn. We descended down the trail we had just come up, back to the canyon edge. After a while of tracing this edge, the trail deposited us onto a road we were to cross. From here we ascended a steep fire road into the hills, which seemed to go on forever in the intense baking heat. We saw only a lone side-by-side on this section of trail, going the other direction. After a long grueling effort we again ended up on the top of a plateau, tracing another canyon's edge. We soon departed from this canyon, snaking between large dirt mounds, a new section of climbing appearing around every corner. At some point we finally crossed the road again, marking the beginning of the pure descent. It was fast, flowy and easy. We came down a dirt ridge and into the plains, gaining velocity towards the car. At the end we had done about 28 miles, our hands were rattled and bodies tired. Nearly 40 miles of mountain biking in the desert heat that day, we were ready to get in the river we had driven over on the way in. We did so, refreshing ourselves and washing off the day. After getting some food in town, we drove back to our campsite, to spend the last night on the road of a very long trip.

## Day 27: Head Back Home | 5/24

The next morning we were up early, and we said goodbye as Weston headed east and I west. We would be seeing each other soon enough, once we were both back and rested. I drove back the many miles and several states between me and home, the long drive barely registering over the excitement to finally be nearing my little family back home. The sun rose up overhead, then drifted back into the rearview, along with the hot desert floor. Soon I was winding through familiar mountains and hills, the temperature slowly dropping. Eventually the ocean came into view, and I realized how long it had been since I had last seen it. I was home in the early afternoon, and greeted the dogs as they lost their minds. I got to hold my girlfriend again, then had to unpack.

This trip was an important and defining one for me, it was the longest I had been away from this small family of mine since I had known any of them. It was the longest I had spent traveling, and was the most independent I think I have ever felt. I learned a lot about myself, about who I was, what I value and seek out in life, what I want to put energy and time into in the future, and who I want to be. This trip had been an obsession of mine, I had pored over my laptop for months, tirelessly planning our days and filling my horizon with goals. I had dreamed of what it would mean if we could accomplish all that we set out to, what kind of a world it could open up. Scaling towers, buttresses and cracks, I now felt like I had turned another page towards being a "real climber".

But as I reflected on my trip with my girlfriend, all of that seemed to pale in comparison to the human connection I had experienced on my trip. Seeing so many of my good friends and family, surviving nearly entirely off their good will and generosity. The personal acclompishment and sense of individualality I felt on my trip were powerful and important, but they were alone in my head, important to no one else. I had trekked along this web of people, some family, some new friends, some old, spanning multiple states and all related by nothing but caring for me. Now, cozy in my home in California, I could still feel the open arms a thousand miles away. I was grateful to be back on our king size bed, even if the dogs are crowding it. I laid back and fell asleep, full with love for the people that make my life so special.
