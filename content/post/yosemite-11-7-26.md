## The Plan

Casey and I met on the top of Cathedral Peak in Santa Barbara in early 2025, where Mason and I were topping out my first multipitch climb. Casey was there with a few others working on local route development, something Casey has done much of in the Santa Barbara area. Despite being an experienced climber and developer, Casey had never been climbing in Yosemite. We had done lots of local climbing together in Santa Barbara, climbing routes that Casey and his friends had put up. We had one failed trip to Tahquitz under our collective belts, where the rain had sent us home early. This fall we started talking about doing another trip together, and ultimately landed on Yosemite as our destination. 

We discussed a few different climbs we were both excited about, and made plans to drive up Thursday afternoon. Soon the day was upon us, and Casey and I swapped shifts at the wheel to drown out the long drive. We rolled into Camp 4 shortly after 9 pm and found our site, unfortunately about as far from the parking lot as was possible. Camp was still lively and loud at this early time in the night, and we quickly set up our tents, popped in our earplugs, and drifted off to sleep. 

## Day 1: East Buttress El Capitan | 11/07

We were up very early the next day at 5:30 am, trying to maximize the short window of sunlight during this time of year and avoid climbing in the darkness that was already less than 12 hours away. We were setting out to climb the East Buttress of El Capitan this day, a long route up the furthest right side of El Cap. We hopped into the car and drove down to the El Cap Meadow, parking directly beneath the massive behemoth. After a short bit spent getting ready; making food and packing gear, we were on the trail and hiking up towards the right side of this massive wall by 6:30 am.

The trail climbed fairly directly to the far right base, meandering through a steep boulder field. To our left we could see a variety of porta-ledges hanging off different lines up the more prominent section of El Cap. The climbers up there were also just beginning their day, starting to make small vertical progress up the wall. When we neared the base of the wall, I could here faint taps in the grass about 20-25 ft from the base of the wall. This noise was created by water drops, falling off the overhanging top of El Cap, thousands of feet above.

Soon we were at the base and began putting on our gear. Before we could begin climbing, we heard the jingling of another party approaching behind us. They got the base just as we were getting ready. One member of the party introduced himself as Jesse, the head of YOSAR, or Yosemite Search and Rescue. He had climbed this route over 20 times before he said, and was taking his friend Bob from North Carolina up the route today. Bob was in his 60's and started climbing just over a decade ago. After some short discussion, we decided to let them start before us as they were planning to link pitches and likely move much quicker than us. Jesse cruised up the Chimney and pulled some strenuous looking moves to get to the first belay. Here he decided against linking these first two pitches, and Bob started climbing up behind him. Once Bob was far enough above, I joined my place in the train and started leading the first pitch.

This pitch started with some difficult and tiring chimneying, which eventually gives way into hand jams in a corner crack. Just before the anchors, the corner crack widens dramatically into a body-width pod before closing back into a small vertical section of thin hands to the ledge with the anchors. Here was where Jesse made some tricky looking moves, stemming in the corner and some marginal holds out to the right. I instead elected to stay wholly in the corner crack, pulling an awkward move to get into the pod and pulling up hard on tenuous thin hand jams to get to the anchors. This pitch was tricky, tough, and tiring, and I was glad to have gotten it clean. Casey followed up behind me, lugging off his harness the heavy pack full of our shoes and water. This pitch gave Casey a difficult time, plunging him head first into the polished, slippery wide climbing that seems to be the signature of the valley.

At the top of the first pitch we were now standing just next to the crux of the climb, watching as Bob fired up it on follow. To the right of our ledge was a large blank, featureless section of wall, about 3 feet wide and 6 feet tall.The crux involved stepping onto this featureless section with the help of miniscule crystals to place a millimeter of rubber on. Up and to the right was a small pin scar, or a tiny section of crack that has been scarred and worn down by gear decades of gear placements, making the edges rounded and the inside flared. Reaching up with the right hand I was able to grab onto this pin scar while stepping on the featureless wall. Then my left hand moves up to a large sloping ledge. Then my right hand moves up to match it. Then move the feet up further, looking for anything they will stick on. Reach up again with the right hand and plunge it into a crack above. Pull up on this and you can stand on the sloping ledge, finally through the crux. 

This movement took me a few tries to figure out, and I took a few swinging falls back into Casey at the belay. The last piece was just above the belay, and the next one was in the crack above and to the right of the featureless slab that defined the crux. My first attempt my feet popped almost immediately. My second attempt I made it almost entirely through, sank the handjam at the end, but then again blew my feet and slid off before I could save myself. After popping off a third time I could feel myself getting pumped, tired and frustrated, anxious about yet another party coming up on our heels. The leader waited in the wide pod below the first pitch anchors, staying out of range of my swinging falls. Luckily, on my fourth go the movement finally seemed trivial, and I stood on top of the crux successful. Around the corner the pitch continued in a sort of water gully with a small splitter finger crack in the back, creating a cool combination of chimneying and small finger locks. 

Casey took the next lead, a short and easy face climbing pitch, traversing out and around the party previously beneath us, now sharing the same belay with us. They were a friendly couple of guys form Catalonia, one of whom was supposedly a climbing guide in the area. We ended up spending much of the climb with them, climbing at relatively the same pace and sharing many anchors. Casey climbed up with pitch with the guide from their party, both finding their way up the featured rock.

From the top of pitch 3 I took the lead again, continuing the route-findy climbing/scrambling up to a large corner. I climbed up into this corner as it steepened, the climbing slowly returning to its more technical nature. Casey followed me up and took the next lead, continuing up the corner and pulling some tricky moves past large ledges. 

{{< post-image image="https://pub-4c7e902ff94f46fba2377f9c95feeba0.r2.dev/yosemite-11-7-26/IMG_2745.JPEG"  alt="ebec p5" lightbox=true >}}
Our Catalan friend leading up our 5th pitch.
{{< /post-image >}}

At the top of the fifth pitch I took the lead, and waited for our Catalan friends to move far enough along the pitch. They warned that this pitch was particularly tough, and I could see the leader was aiding through the majority of it. I started leading once they were out of the way, and found myself quickly agreeing with their synopsis. The short pitch stepped out onto an arete, where you found yourself truly on the East Buttress of El Cap. To the left the massive sea of granite spread out into the distance, culminating at the long line of The Nose. To the right the valley snaked into the distance, Half Dome standing like a snow-capped barrier to another world. Below was nothing but air, hundreds of feet back to the base of the climb. A few easy slab moves up this arete deposit the climber right at the base of an angling crack system. This system didn't look so bad from below, but now standing at it, I spent a decent while looking for where to put my feet and hands to move through the next section. After a bit of analysis, I plugged a nut into the crack and moved past it, getting jams and feet where I could. I managed to get through this pitch cleanly, and felt it took a great deal of remaining calm, taking the time to find good gear and hand and foot holds, and moving confidently past my gear. All things I have struggled with in the past, and that felt good to be improving on.

From this pitch we were now at what I had expected to be the mental and physical meat of the climb; a pitch of wide and physical climbing with supposedly unprotected sections of technical movement. I climbed up into the wide crack, making use of various jams where I could find them and employing a good bit of chimneying. Eventually I plugged my last large piece into the wide crack in the corner, then moved leftwards away from it, and up into unprotected territory. There were some engaging moves on small holds, far enough above my last piece to where I really did not want to fall.  I never felt too gripped or worried, and managed to get to the top of the pitch fairly quickly. Poor extension of pieces below made for some rope drag at the top which made the final unprotected 5.9 moves especially exciting.

{{< post-image image="https://pub-4c7e902ff94f46fba2377f9c95feeba0.r2.dev/yosemite-11-7-26/IMG_2763.JPEG"  alt="ebec p7" lightbox=true >}}

{{< /post-image >}}

From here Casey took the lead back, snaking us around a short and easy traverse-y pitch. Casey kept the lead for the next pitch as well, taking us up a 5.8 R bit of face climbing. We were on the heels of the Catalan party ahead of us, and trying to move quickly after I realized I had forgotten my headlamp in the car. Casey waited on a ledge with them until they were moving ahead, then finished his lead.

{{< post-image image="https://pub-4c7e902ff94f46fba2377f9c95feeba0.r2.dev/yosemite-11-7-26/IMG_2781.JPEG"  alt="ebec p9" lightbox=true >}}
Casey leading our 9th pitch, sitting on the ledge and waiting for our Catalan friend to start moving.
{{< /post-image >}}

From here I climbed one long rope-stretching pitch to the top, through gradually easing terrain. At the top the slabs leveled out, and I stuck in a belay to bring Casey up. The sun was setting just as we reached the top, bathing the face of half dome in a orange glaze. We made quick work of packing up our gear and started down the East Ledges descent from El Capitan, Casey generously letting me use his headlamp. Fixed ropes made the series of six rappels rather painless, and soon the boulder field and slabs were giving way to an actual trail. As we dropped into the Nutcracker parking area we heard loud rustling in the bushes, and I turned to see a couple pairs of eyes staring back at us through the forest. A couple bears rummaging for food, we were glad to be back on asphalt by the time we saw them.

A short walk back to the car under the constellation of climbers' lights on El Cap, and we were headed back to camp. Casey had brought some pizza dough he had made at home, and we feasted on a luxurious campfire pizza before sharing some with our neighbors and climbing into our tents.

## Day 2: Central Pillar of Frenzy | 11/08

The next day we woke up rather leisurely, a welcome change in pace from the pre-dawn rise of the day before. Today we were planning to climb Central Pillar of Frenzy, a 5 pitch 5.9 regarded as one of the best 5.9's in Yosemite, if not the world. This climb had been on my horizon for quite some time, as I've been ticking off classic 5.9's around the American west for the past year or so. This one specifically felt similar to what Serenity Crack now is for me; a classic and outstanding line which I wanted to work my way towards climbing in good style, rather than hopping on it as soon as possible just to flounder. Many of my Yosemite partners had done this climb several times, and so it had sat on my todo list for a while. Now, with it being Casey's first time in the valley, and me finally feeling fairly confident on this kind of terrain, it seemed the perfect opportunity to get on this climb. 

Around 9 am I ran into a party of 3 in the Camp 4 parking lot discussing how to get to Central Pillar. I gave them directions and told them we might be not too far behind them. Casey and I made some breakfast and tea and went for a short walk to check out the freshly rejuvenated Yosemite Falls. Once we returned we headed to the car and drove towards the Meadow, the valley now fully warmed by the sun.

{{< post-image image="https://pub-4c7e902ff94f46fba2377f9c95feeba0.r2.dev/yosemite-11-7-26/IMG_2815.JPEG"  alt="falls" lightbox=true >}}
The first bit of snowmelt feeding the falls this season.
{{< /post-image >}}

From the parking area at the meadow, Central Pillar is less than half a mile of distance and 200 feet of elevation gain. We were at the base quickly, where the party of three was getting started. Their leader climbed up the first pitch, which was supposedly the crux of climb. He took his time and made it to the first anchors without issue. His two followers started to come up behind him, each being belayed one at a time.

After a bit of discussion this party graciously offered for us to pass them. We were more than fine waiting to give them space, but as their belaying took more time and more parties showed up behind us, we became very amicable to their offer. I took the lead for the first pitch and climbed upwards through the tricky chimney, getting jams wherever I could find them. This pitch was tricky and involved switching between traditional crack climbing and chimneying in limited stances. Towards the top I found myself stuck in the chimney, unable to turn my body in the direction I needed. Eventually I was able to pop an arm out to the side, and pull upwards on the good hand jam. I was now at the anchors with the party of 3, where Casey followed up to join us.

{{< post-image image="https://pub-4c7e902ff94f46fba2377f9c95feeba0.r2.dev/yosemite-11-7-26/IMG_2273.JPEG"  alt="cp p1" lightbox=true >}}
Party at the P1 anchor. From left to right; Unberto, Justin, Sam, Casey, me.
{{< /post-image >}}

Now we were past the traffic jam with fortunately no one above us. Casey took the lead and fired up the second pitch, a direct line up a set of parallel cracks. This pitch was insanely good, with every type of perfect jam from fingers to thin hands to perfect hands. I cruised my way up and was soon with Casey at the belay. From here I took the third pitch, which continued up a perfect hand-sized crack, to a large roof above us. I climbed up and to the imposing roof, pulling on good hand jams to get through it. After placing a high foot in the roof I stood up and was looking at the terrain above it. 30 feet of fist-sized crack continued upwards, to the next set of anchors. I climbed up, walking my last two big pieces until I was close enough to the anchor to climb to it. Casey followed after me, commenting on how stellar these pitches were.

{{< post-image image="https://pub-4c7e902ff94f46fba2377f9c95feeba0.r2.dev/yosemite-11-7-26/IMG_2837.JPEG"  alt="cp p3" lightbox=true >}}
Casey in the fist jams, just after coming through the roof.
{{< /post-image >}}

The 4th and 5th pitch continued up more systems of perfect and parallel cracks until we reached the top of the climb. The rock continues upwards for many more pitches, but the quality of the line diminishes so much that only these first five pitches are commonly climbed. Once both at the 5th pitch anchors, we got ready to rappel to the ground, frothing over how good the route had been. I'm not sure that any route I've ever climbed has left me with such a sense of euphoria and satisfaction; images of certain locks and moves are still drifting around in my head nearly a week later, like the memory of an outrageously good meal I've had.

After a series of rappels to the base, we were back on the ground and headed to the car well before dark. We opted out of cooking again and drove to Curry Village for some pizza. We shared a table and made friends with a nice guy visiting from Chicago, and saw our party of 3 friends from Central Pillar. After a long line and long wait for some good pizza and beer, we headed back to camp to call it a night.

## Day 3: Drive Home

In my beer induced confidence the night before I had brainstormed climbing ideas for the next day, but when I awoke on Sunday it was very clear that neither of us would be climbing. My fingers were sore and achey, toes sensitive and painful, and random chimney related bruises littered my body. I spent the morning packing up, and went for a short walk to check out some boulders while I waited for Casey to wake up. My friend Juan was also staying in Camp 4, climbing with a friend of his. I said hi and caught up with him. Soon Casey was up and we were ready to go, both eager to get started on the long drive. We headed out early and were back to Santa Barbara before 3 pm.

Yosemite is such an amazing place, and I was so grateful to be able to share Casey's first experience in this amazing place with him. We got on some awesome climbs, both very distinctly different representations of climbing in the Valley. This was my 8th time climbing here and I still feel like I've just barely scratched the surface of the climbing here, and I'm eagerly awaiting the snow melting this Spring.
